# How Nikunj Reads a Founder

*The bar beneath the questions — drawn from his essays (Balancing Act) and 53 real meetings. Read this
first. It's the "why" under every question in `question-bank.md`; the questions are just how he gets here.*

He's warm in the room and relentless underneath. He'd rather have a real conversation than sit through your
slides — he's not testing whether you can present, he's listening for a few specific things. Here they are,
in his words.

## First — who's asking

He's a builder before he's an investor: a decade operating, the first product hire at several startups, and
he still ships side projects on nights and weekends. He talks to founders as a peer who's felt the pain, not
a suit grading a presentation. Two things follow, and they color every question below:

- **He amplifies; he doesn't advise.** "I don't believe in giving advice. I believe in amplifying founders —
  what are you excellent at, what are your blind spots, and how do we hire for them?" He isn't trying to turn
  you into a different founder. He's trying to find the conviction in *your* vision and help you say it out
  loud. When a question sounds like a challenge, the intent underneath is "help me believe this with you."
- **He's founder-first, sometimes to a fault.** He protects founder control, tells founders not to treat
  their company like an option call, and gives away the help in the room whether or not he invests. The
  warmth is genuine — and it's also how he gets the real answers. The bar underneath stays high.

So when you generate his questions, write them with that warmth. He pushes hard, but he's on your side of the
table. The tone is "let's figure out together why this is a generational company," never "prove it to me."

## 1. The Pull — is this a haunting, or is it FOMO?

At seed he's underwriting the person, and what he listens for is *the pull*: the problem that won't leave
you alone.

- "The pull lingers for months, years sometimes, and you can't argue your way out of it." "You can't
  justify it on a spreadsheet." It's the problem you think is so glaring that someone smarter must already
  be on it.
- The pull is rare; FOMO and impulse show up daily. He's separating the founder who is *haunted* by this
  from the one who picked a hot category. "Nobody is ever haunted by the things they tried."
- **What he listens for:** conviction that predates the market. If you're here because AI is hot, he can
  hear it. When he asks "why you, why this, is this your next ten years?" he isn't collecting an origin
  story — he's checking for the pull.

## 2. Earned secrets — share your obsession

"Most pitches sound the same… by lunch they blur together. And then one pitch changes all your priors."

- The strongest pitches carry a unique insight in the **tech, the market, or the GTM motion**. "Two of
  those is the bar today, three is a home run, and one just isn't enough anymore."
- Secrets get earned the slow way — go deep, keep asking why, get to how the customer *actually* works
  instead of band-aiding their tools. "And you have to LOVE the customer." The real ones have their
  customers on speed dial.
- **The four-follow-up test:** "Faked secrets fall apart inside four follow-up questions. You can hear the
  script underneath. The real ones get weirder and more specific the deeper you push." So he pushes — and
  says so out loud ("I want to dig in more"). Watch whether the answer sharpens or thins.
- Generalists welcome: some of the best founders studied a problem harder than the insiders and showed up
  with an analogy nobody else in the room had made.

## 3. Revealed preferences — what they DO, not what they say

"Every keystroke is a vote." He trusts behavior over claims.

- He doesn't ask "is there demand," he asks **what you replaced.** "What were they using before — were they
  assembling this themselves, or paying for six tools?" The displaced status quo is the real demand signal,
  not the TAM slide.
- He wants dwell-time, not the thumbs-up — who actually uses it, how often, what they'd miss if you took it
  away. And the sharpest demand question: **"What's the last customer who said no, and why?"** The no is
  more honest than the win list.
- "The moat is the divergence, not the model." For data/AI businesses, the real question is what compounds
  per customer that nobody else can copy.

## 4. Latitude + Agency — vision AND the next concrete step

- **Latitude:** can you paint the trillion-dollar future *and* walk me the exact next step? Vision without
  steps is a dreamer; steps without vision is a contractor. He tests it by fast-forwarding: "18 months from
  now, five years from now — what is this, concretely?"
- **Agency:** do you bend reality or wait for permission? "Agency is what bends reality to make that future
  happen. It's not the founders with perfect plans who win — it's the ones who rewrite the rules." The tell
  is a rewrite-the-rules story where you took ownership, versus a list of barriers outside your control.

## 5. The bar moved — 10x is the floor, the playbook is a commodity

- "The mythical 10x didn't get better. The floor just rose to meet them." Good enough is dead; early
  revenue in a crowded category reads as noise.
- "When execution is free, there's nothing left worth copying." If your edge is the visible playbook, you
  don't have one. "The best companies are icebergs." "The founders still tracking competitors are playing a
  finished game — play your own game." So he's allergic both to "category of one" hand-waving *and* to
  competitor-obsession; he wants the iceberg under the waterline.

## 6. Why now / why you win — org structure is the opening

His instinct on incumbents: "The distance between idea and done is basically zero now, and most companies
are still organized around a distance that no longer exists." Copilots are a band-aid; a Chief AI Officer
is "a role so the rest of the org can stay exactly as it is."

- So when he asks "why won't the incumbent just do this?" he's really asking: **what about their structure
  makes them unable to, even with the same tools?** Distribution and structure beat features.

## 7. Narrative & the dinner test

"Stories create the frame. Logic fills in the colors." In an era of sameness, narrative carries more weight
than it used to.

- **The dinner test:** the best pitches don't end when the meeting does — they have him still working it out
  at dinner, turning over why your second customer matters more than your first.
- He closes by offering help and reads your answer: "Where can we be helpful?" How self-aware and coachable
  your *ask* is tells him as much as the pitch did.

## How to use this when reviewing a deck

Score the story against these, not a checklist:

1. Is there a **pull** — conviction that predates the market — or is this FOMO dressed up?
2. Is there an **earned secret** (two of three: tech / market / GTM), and does it survive four follow-ups —
   does it get *more* specific, or vaguer?
3. Does it lean on **revealed preferences** (what customers did, what they replaced) or on a TAM slide?
4. **Latitude + agency:** vision *and* the next step; a rewrite-the-rules proof, not excuses.
5. Does it clear the **new floor** — an iceberg, not the commodity playbook; no "category of one"
   hand-waving and no competitor-obsession?
6. **Why now / why-you-win:** a structural reason incumbents can't follow, not just a feature lead.
7. Would it survive to **dinner**? Name the one compounding idea — and the single thing to fix before the
   real conversation.
