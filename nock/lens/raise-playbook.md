# How the Raise Itself Gets Read

*From his essay on fundraising in a consensus market (August 2026). `principles.md` is how **he** reads a
founder; this is how the **market** reads your raise — the process mechanics that shape a round before the
diligence does. Load-bearing whenever a deck carries an ask slide, or a founder asks how to run the raise.*

## 0. The consensus market — legibility is the game

"This is the most consensus market I have seen in a while. If you're hot, you're really hot. If you're not,
you're not. There's almost nobody left in the middle."

- In a consensus market most VCs aren't doing independent work — "they're reading signals off you and
  comparing notes." The error of omission is too high for funds to miss the hot deal. "Whether your company
  is actually good comes later, sometimes much later."
- So the most successful founders are the most **legible**. It started as a joke; it's basically the game.
  Everything below is about what gets read, and how.

## 1. Who you talk to at a firm

- **Associates and principals are really great** — but look at what they've backed recently to understand
  their leverage and standing in the firm. Newer, worked on great recent deals, understands your space?
  Talk to them, and get a warm intro to *them* too. If not, they might be on the way out.
- Figure out quickly whether the fund is actually interested or just giving you lip service.
- **Once you're assigned to someone in the CRM, you're stuck with that person.** "Wars have been fought over
  deal attribution. You think I'm joking but no."
- Eventually you have to get to the **GPs — the capital-P partners with actual authority, not just the
  title.** A warm intro changes everything: angels are great for it, and a founder they've already backed is
  even better. "They'll run onto a plane to talk to you. Schedules vanish. You don't get passed to an
  associate (usually)."

## 2. The ask — you can't take the number back

- Ask for too much and they look at you skeptically; too little and you're not ambitious enough. Too little
  dilution, it's not worth it; too much, it's too good of a deal. "There's no science here, but come
  prepared with a resemblance of a plan for why you're raising that much."
- **The number you say out loud matters far more than you think, because you can't take it back.** Say $30M,
  fail to get it, come back at $20M — "it doesn't work that way. It seeds a doubt that you weren't able to
  fundraise, which scares investors even more, and it shows poor judgement on why you needed that money in
  the first place."
- His standing advice: **aim a tad lower so you can actually raise the amount, ideally in a competitive
  scenario with multiple term sheets.** "It's still an art. A hard one."

## 3. Price & ownership — VCs don't want a "deal"

- The number one thing founders don't understand: **if the price is too good, it begets even more
  questions** — like why is the price not higher. "It's weird but it's true."
- **No established lead fund will do under 10% dilution, so don't even ask.** The floor is set because
  someone inside that fund is showing their partners how this one returns the fund. (Exceptions exist; with
  insane leverage, go for it.)
- **Never compare on what your competitor got.** "It's the single best way to tank the deal. You have no
  idea how that deal actually got done" — keep it privately as a comp, never as an argument.

## 4. The story bar — obvious, or already lost

- It's easier than ever to raise a seed and much harder to raise an A: **there's no revenue number that gets
  you a Series A anymore.** The bar is whether what you're building is *obvious* — "as soon as you're
  justifying the size of your opportunity, you've probably already lost."
- So share your **unfair advantages in product, tech, or GTM — ideally all three** — grounded in your
  company, your people, what you're observing. (Same bar as the earned-secret test in `principles.md` §2:
  two of three is the bar, three is a home run.)
- There's probably a competitor already doing what you're doing. **Your unique insight is what gives a VC
  confidence on why you'll win anyway.**

## 5. Great hires are the Mandate of Heaven

- "People severely discount how much hiring great folks gives you the Mandate of Heaven in a fundraise."
  Hire great people for the company anyway — but if you have great new hires, **showcase them in the
  pitch.** "I'm totally shocked how few companies do this."
- It also helps VCs underwrite the downside: worst case, the company gets bought for the talent.

## 6. Small things get read

- **Your traction slide should show the current month**, even if you started fundraising last month. "June
  numbers in August smell like stale goods."
- When anyone asks about your timeline, **the answer is two weeks. It's always two weeks.**

## 7. You're being read even when you're not in the room

- The valley — and New York especially — is insanely small. "If you tell a fund you have a term sheet from
  someone, it's literally a text away." VC is a co-opetition; everyone talks to everyone. All decks leak.
- **Don't lie, and don't share anything you don't want shared.** Even if the upstanding VC keeps it close,
  another one won't.

## 8. Vibes — maybe more important than any of this

- **Be you.** Showcase what you're excited by and highlight where you need help — "we thrive on helping."
  (This is the same read as his close in `principles.md` §7: the quality of your *ask* is signal.)
- "You can play the silly game of negging and acting like someone you're not, but trust me, it works far
  less than you think." Hype buys eyeballs and attention; it's not the end-all.

## 9. Not in a hot sector? Strap in

- The market "will read you top to bottom and still get it wrong." Even the best companies struggled to
  raise a round — look at Anthropic.
- Share **what makes you a great contrarian bet** — everyone is balancing their portfolio, and "you never
  know where you'll find a champion, even if you strike out with the top 100 firms."
- **Figure out your default-alive path.** Valuation markers come and go; a brand and a big number are nice
  for the ego. "What matters is fuel in the tank to keep your ambition high."
- "All it takes is one yes."

## How to use this when reviewing a deck

Run the raise-mechanics read alongside the story read:

1. **The ask slide:** is there a number *and* a resemblance of a plan for why that number? Is it sized to be
   actually raisable — ideally competitively — or is it a number they'll have to walk back?
2. **Price/ownership sanity:** any implied ask under 10% dilution to a lead, or a "great deal" framing that
   would beget more questions instead of interest?
3. **Comp anchoring:** does the deck (or the founder's framing) lean on what a competitor raised or priced
   at? Flag it — it's the single best way to tank the deal.
4. **Traction recency:** does the traction slide show the current month, or stale goods?
5. **Team slide:** are there great recent hires — and are they showcased, or buried?
6. **Legibility / obviousness:** is the opportunity presented as obvious, or is the deck *justifying* the
   size of the opportunity? Justifying = probably already lost.
7. **If the sector isn't hot:** is there a contrarian-bet framing and a default-alive path, or only a
   hot-market story?
